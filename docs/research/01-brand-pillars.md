# Brand-building pillars and onboarding workflow

*Research basis for Waterlily onboarding. Compiled 2026-10-06. Sources marked [verified] were fetched and checked. Sources marked [secondary] are summaries of a book or model that could not be read in the original. Statistics are left out on purpose, because none of the primary sources gives one that matters here.*

## 1. Company brand-strategy frameworks and their components

| Framework | Origin | What it contributes | Components |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Golden Circle** | Simon Sinek, TEDxPugetSound, 2009 [verified][1] | Purpose sits at the centre and comes first | Why (purpose or belief), How (process), What (product); communicate from the inside out |
| **Brand Identity Planning Model** | David Aaker, *Building Strong Brands* (1996); summarised by Prophet [verified][2][3] | Core identity versus extended identity | 2–5 core identity elements, 3–5 extended elements, optional brand essence; extended elements help decide whether a new initiative is on brand. Four perspectives: product, organisation, person, symbol [secondary][4] |
| **Brand Identity Prism** | Jean-Noël Kapferer, *The New Strategic Brand Management* (Kogan Page) [verified][5] | Identity as six facets | Physique, Personality, Culture, Relationship, Reflection, Self-image [secondary][6] |
| **Customer-Based Brand Equity (CBBE)** | Kevin Lane Keller, MSI Working Paper 01-107, 2001 [verified][7][8] | Brand building as a sequence of steps | Identity, then meaning, then response, then relationship; six building blocks: salience, performance, imagery, judgments, feelings, resonance [8][9] |
| **Brand archetypes** | Mark & Pearson, *The Hero and the Outlaw* (2001), after Jung [verified][10][11] | A shortcut to personality and story | 12 archetypes in four motivational groups; use group membership only, because secondary sources label the quadrants differently |
| **Positioning** | April Dunford, *Obviously Awesome* (2019) [verified][12] | Positioning built from parts that depend on each other | Competitive alternatives, unique capabilities, differentiated value, target customers, market category, worked out in that order |
| **Onlyness / Zag** | Marty Neumeier, *Zag* (2006) [verified][13] | A strict differentiation test | "Our brand is the *only* ___ that ___." |
| **Value Proposition Canvas** | Strategyzer [verified][14] | Matches the offer to what the customer needs | Jobs, pains and gains; products, pain relievers and gain creators |
| **Jobs to be Done** | Christensen et al., HBR 2016 [verified][15] | Defines the audience by the job they need done, not by demographics | Functional, social and emotional dimensions |
| **Message architecture** | Margot Bloomstein; BrandSort [verified][16] | Turns strategy into a ranked list of messages | Card sort: who we are / who we aspire to be / who we are not, then rank |
| **Tone dimensions** | NN/g (Kate Moran) [verified][17] | Makes voice measurable | Formal–casual, serious–funny, respectful–irreverent, matter-of-fact–enthusiastic |

**Pillars that appear in most of these models:**
- Purpose and vision
- Values
- Audience and the job they need done
- Positioning and differentiation
- Personality, archetype and voice
- Promise and value proposition
- Message hierarchy
- Visual and verbal identity

## 2. Personal branding: frameworks and how they differ

- **Origin.** Tom Peters, "The Brand Called You" (1997). In his 25th-anniversary note he says the idea was widely misread as image-crafting; he meant delivering real value through excellent work [verified][18].
- **Avery and Greenwald, HBR (2023), seven steps** [verified][19]:
  1. Define your purpose.
  2. Audit your brand equity, including how others see you.
  3. Construct your narrative.
  4. Embody the brand in every interaction.
  5. Communicate it.
  6. Socialise it, so influential people repeat your stories.
  7. Re-evaluate every year.
- **Dorie Clark, HBR (2011)** [verified][20]:
  1. Define your destination.
  2. Use your points of difference.
  3. Write a narrative that connects past work to current goals.
  4. Reintroduce yourself to your network.
  5. Prove your worth through content and associations.

**How a personal brand differs from a company brand** (synthesis):
1. **Identity is found, not chosen.** The raw material is a real person's history, strengths and values, so discovery leans on extracting their story.
2. **Perception has to be audited from outside.** Both HBR pieces require finding out how others currently see you.
3. **The narrative carries the brand,** rather than a category claim.
4. **Proof comes from work and from other people:** visible work, endorsements and who you associate with.
5. **Founder brands are hybrids.** The founder's brand and the company's brand affect each other. GV's brand sprint requires the CEO in the room [21]. Treat the founder as a separate profile linked to the company (inference: no authoritative founder-brand framework was found).

## 3. How agencies and strategists run discovery workshops

**GV Three-Hour Brand Sprint** [verified][21]:
1. 20-year roadmap
2. What, How, Why
3. Top three values
4. Top three audiences ("Whose opinion do you care about?")
5. Personality sliders (Friend–Authority, Young–Classic, Playful–Serious, Mass appeal–Elite)
6. Competitive 2x2 (Classic–Modern × Expressive–Reserved)

**Attend The Way / BrandCraft (Adam Charlton)** [verified][22]:
- A 21-question questionnaire covering background, customers and competition, vision, values, personality and mission.
- Then two 1–2 hour workshops: brand audit, service map, positioning matrix, vision roadmap, value wheel (20–25 values narrowed to 6), personality sliders, archetype cards, moodboards.

**HubSpot buyer-persona interview guide** [verified][23]:
- Question areas: background, company, role, goals, learning sources, buying behaviour, values, pain points, tools.
- Method: 30–45 minute interviews, 3–10 of them; refresh personas about twice a year.

**Bloomstein BrandSort** [verified][16]: card sort into is / aspires to be / is not, then rank.

**Other templates** (vendor marketing, less authoritative): HoneyBook, Wayfront, ManyRequests [24].

## 4. Cold start: defining a brand with little or no content

Most discovery inputs live in the founder's head, the market or customers, not in a content archive. All of these work with no existing content:

1. **Interview-led discovery.** Run the GV sprint and Charlton questions as a guided conversation [21][22].
2. **Founder or personal story extraction** from a CV or LinkedIn profile plus an interview [19][20].
3. **Proto personas, labelled as assumptions.** NN/g says these are quick for aligning a team but "often inaccurate"; validate them with 5–30 interviews [verified][25].
4. **Customer and prospect interviews, where any exist.** JTBD switch interviews (forces of progress) [26]; *The Mom Test* for unbiased signal [27].
5. **Competitor and category analysis** from public data: Dunford's alternatives, GV's 2x2, Neumeier's onlyness test. AI can research these.
6. **Self-selection exercises:** value card sorts, personality and tone sliders, archetype picks [16][17][21][22].

## 5. Recommended guided workflow (9 steps)

Every step works with no existing content (ZERO-OK). Each step uses the outputs of the earlier ones.

| # | Step | Ask the user | AI can infer or draft | Output |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Intake and brand type** | Company, person or founder? Stage? Links? 12-month goals? | First summary from any links; gap list of which pillars have evidence | Brand profile and pillar-coverage checklist |
| 2 | **Origin and founder story** | Why did you start? What was broken? Turning points? | Story beats and candidate proof points | Origin story and proof-point bank |
| 3 | **Purpose, vision and values** | What/How/Why; 5/10/20-year roadmap; rank value cards | Draft mission and vision; propose values, citing which answers each comes from | Mission/vision and 3 ranked values |
| 4 | **Audience and jobs** | "Whose opinion do you care about?" Real customers or fans? | Proto personas with jobs, pains and gains, **labelled as assumptions**; validation interview scripts | 1–3 personas and a validation script |
| 5 | **Competitive landscape** | Who are you compared with? What would people do if you didn't exist? | Competitor research and a 2x2 | Alternatives map |
| 6 | **Positioning and value proposition** | Confirm capabilities, value and category | Positioning options, onlyness line, fit check; flag claims with no proof | Positioning, value proposition, promise |
| 7 | **Personality, archetype and voice** | Sliders, archetype cards, we-are / we-are-not | Sample copy at each slider setting so the user chooses by example; measure existing content if any | Voice and tone guide |
| 8 | **Message architecture and story** | Card sort and rank | Message house: core message, 3–5 pillars, proof points; pitches; bios | Messaging house |
| 9 | **Identity and launch kit** | Moodboards, any existing assets, which channels | Visual direction, 30-day calendar mapped to the pillars | Brand guidelines and content plan |

**Ongoing loop:** re-validate personas as real interviews and engagement data arrive [23][25], and re-assess the whole brand once a year [19].

**Design notes:**
1. Every AI suggestion shows where it came from, because inferred personas can lock in wrong assumptions.
2. Founders get two linked profiles, personal and company, sharing one story bank.
3. Steps 3, 6 and 7 are decision points the user approves before anything downstream is generated.

**Uncertainties:**
- Kapferer facets, Aaker's four perspectives and Keller's block names rely on secondary summaries or the paper PDF.
- Archetype quadrant labels differ between sources.
- No peer-reviewed comparison of cold-start methods was found; the step order is a synthesis.
